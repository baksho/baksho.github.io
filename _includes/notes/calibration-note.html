Authors: Chuan Guo, Geoff Pleiss, Yu Sun, and Kilian Q. Weinberger.

Venue: Proceedings of the 34th International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML), 2017, PMLR 70

DOI: 10.5555/3305381.3305518

Paper type: Empirical research paper with methodological analysis.

Problem and Motivation

Modern neural networks achieve high classification accuracy, but their predicted probabilities do not necessarily reflect how often their predictions are correct. This problem is known as confidence miscalibration.

For example, among predictions made with 90% confidence, a well-calibrated model should be correct approximately 90% of the time. An overconfident model may assign 90% confidence to predictions that are correct substantially less often.

The authors argue that accurate confidence estimates matter when model predictions inform consequential decisions, such as medical diagnosis or autonomous driving. A system needs to know not only what it predicts, but also how much confidence to place in that prediction.

The central research questions are:

  • Why are modern neural networks frequently miscalibrated?
  • How do architectural choices and training procedures affect calibration?
  • Which post-processing methods can improve calibration without unnecessarily sacrificing predictive accuracy?

Core contributions

The paper makes four principal contributions.

  1. Identifies miscalibration in modern neural networks. The authors show that high classification accuracy does not guarantee well-calibrated probabilities.
  2. Studies factors affecting calibration. They investigate the effects of network depth, width, weight decay, and Batch Normalization.
  3. Evaluates multiple calibration methods. The study compares histogram binning, isotonic regression, Bayesian Binning into Quantiles (BBQ), Platt scaling variants, and temperature scaling.
  4. Demonstrates the effectiveness of temperature scaling. The authors find that a simple, single-parameter transformation often calibrates predictions as well as or better than more complex alternatives.

Methodology

The paper combines controlled analyses of neural-network training with experiments on image and document classification.

Measuring calibration

The authors primarily evaluate calibration using Expected Calibration Error (ECE). Predictions are grouped into confidence bins, and the difference between average confidence and observed accuracy is calculated for each bin. ECE aggregates these differences, weighted by the proportion of samples in each bin.

Their main results table uses 15 bins. ECE is reported as a percentage, with lower values indicating better calibration.

Calibration methods

The authors evaluate several post-processing approaches using a held-out validation set.

  • Histogram binning: Divides predictions into bins and assigns each bin an empirically estimated confidence.
  • Isotonic regression: Learns a flexible, monotonic mapping from uncalibrated probabilities to calibrated probabilities.
  • BBQ: Averages predictions across candidate binning schemes using Bayesian model averaging.
  • Platt scaling: Fits a logistic transformation to the model's outputs.
  • Vector and matrix scaling: Extend Platt scaling to multiclass classification using more parameters.
  • Temperature scaling: Divides the logits by a single positive scalar \( T \) before applying softmax:

\[ {\hat{p} }_{k}= \frac{\exp ({z}_{k}/T)}{\sum_{j} \exp ({z}_{j}/T)} \]

The temperature is optimized on the validation set using negative log-likelihood (NLL). Because dividing all logits by the same positive scalar preserves their ordering, temperature scaling does not change the predicted class or classification accuracy.

Experimental setup

The paper evaluates image classification datasets including CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet, Caltech-UCSD Birds, Stanford Cars, and SVHN. Document classification experiments use 20 Newsgroups, Reuters, and the Stanford Sentiment Treebank.

Architectures include ResNets, Wide ResNets, DenseNets, Deep Averaging Networks, and TreeLSTMs. For the Birds and Cars datasets, networks pretrained on ImageNet are fine-tuned.

The authors follow the original papers' training procedures and hyperparameters where specified. Calibration is fitted using validation data and evaluated on held-out test data, with the experiments assuming that the training, validation, and test sets are drawn from the same distribution.

Key Results

The following values are taken directly from Table 1 of the paper. All are ECE percentages, using 15 bins.

Dataset and model Before calibration Temperature scaling Best ECE among listed methods
CIFAR-10, ResNet-110 4.60% 0.83% 0.54% (BBQ)
CIFAR-100, ResNet-110 16.53% 1.26% 1.26% (temperature scaling)
CIFAR-100, ResNet-110 with stochastic depth 12.67% 0.96% 0.90% (vector scaling)
ImageNet, DenseNet-161 6.28% 1.99% 1.99% (temperature scaling)
20 Newsgroups, DAN 8.02% 4.11% 3.60% (histogram binning)
Reuters, DAN 0.85% 0.91% 0.66% (vector scaling)

DAN = Deep Averaging Network. All comparisons use the values reported in the paper.

The results support several conclusions.

  • Modern networks can be substantially miscalibrated. On CIFAR-100, the standard ResNet-110 has an ECE of 16.53% before calibration.
  • Temperature scaling is consistently effective, particularly for image classification. It reduces the CIFAR-100 ResNet-110 ECE from 16.53% to 1.26%.
  • More complex calibration is not necessarily better. Temperature scaling frequently outperforms vector and matrix scaling despite using far fewer parameters.
  • Calibration is dataset-dependent. On Reuters, the original ECE is already 0.85%, and temperature scaling does not improve it.
  • Calibration and accuracy can behave differently during training. The authors report that, on CIFAR-100, test error decreases from approximately 29% to 27% during a period when NLL overfits. This is a figure-based result reported in the text, not a universal relationship.

The paper also reports that temperature scaling is computationally inexpensive: its optimization can converge in approximately 10 iterations with a conjugate-gradient solver, taking a fraction of a second on the hardware considered by the authors.

Limitations and Open Questions

The paper identifies several unresolved issues:

  • The reasons why modern architectural and training choices improve accuracy while worsening calibration are not fully understood.
  • The study assumes that training, validation, and test data come from the same distribution.
  • Calibration measurements may depend on the dataset split and the chosen binning scheme.
  • Flexible calibration methods such as matrix scaling can overfit validation data, particularly when the number of classes is large.
  • Temperature scaling is not uniformly effective across all datasets, as illustrated by Reuters.

Personal perspective

The paper establishes an important distinction in machine learning: classification accuracy and confidence reliability are separate properties. A neural network can predict the correct class more often while producing probability estimates that are less trustworthy.

Its central practical contribution is temperature scaling, a simple post-processing method that often substantially improves calibration without changing the predicted classes.

For researchers working on trustworthy ML, the paper provides a foundational starting point for understanding post-hoc calibration. As a matter of fact, this paper was one of the starting points for my Master’s thesis as well. Its main caveat is that successful calibration on a held-out test distribution does not, by itself, establish reliability under distribution shift or provide a complete account of predictive uncertainty.

Following points are my personal assessment:

  1. ECE depends on the evaluation design. ECE is useful but depends on how confidence bins are constructed. A low ECE under one binning scheme does not establish that probabilities are reliable under every evaluation criterion.
  2. Calibration on in-distribution data is not sufficient for deployment. The paper's same-distribution assumption limits what its results establish about distribution shift. A model calibrated on familiar data may become miscalibrated when the input distribution changes.
  3. Calibration is not the same as uncertainty quantification. Temperature scaling adjusts confidence in the predicted classes; it does not directly provide a distribution over plausible models or fully characterize epistemic uncertainty.
  4. Better calibration does not guarantee better decisions. The experiments focus on classification confidence. They do not establish that calibration improves downstream decision-making, clinical outcomes, or safety in a deployed system.
  5. The evidence is strongest for the evaluated benchmarks. The results cover several architectures and datasets, but they do not establish that temperature scaling is universally optimal across all tasks, distributions, or modern model families.

NOTE: The main paper reports dataset splits, model families, evaluation metrics, and the general calibration procedure. It refers to supplementary sections for additional metrics, error tables, and reliability diagrams, which I have not gone through for the purpose of my research and hence has not been verified.
