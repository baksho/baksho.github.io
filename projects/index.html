Research profile

My research interests lie at the intersection of mathematical machine learning, trustworthy AI, and computational biology. I am interested in developing principled methods for predictive reliability, uncertainty quantification, calibration, and interpretability, and in understanding the behavior of learning systems through theoretical analysis and reproducible computational experiments.

My projects span end-to-end calibration, neural-network generalization, uncertainty estimation under distribution shift, causal discovery, interpretable cancer drug-response prediction, and AI-assisted biomedical hypothesis generation. Together, they motivate my doctoral research goal: to develop mathematically grounded and practically useful machine-learning methods that support reliable scientific discovery, particularly in computational biology and biomedical research.

Master's thesis

End-to-End Calibration: Theory and Empirical Evaluation in Biomedical Context

Institute for Statistics and Foundations of Machine Learning, TU Braunschweig. Supervisor: Prof. Dr. Nicole Mücke; second examiner: Prof. Dr. Tim Kacprowski.
Research area: Trustworthy ML, Predictive Calibration, Uncertainty Quantification, Biomedical ML

Research motivation

Modern neural networks can produce highly confident predictions without corresponding empirical reliability. Post-hoc calibration methods can improve confidence estimates after training, but they do not put predictive reliability into the learning objective itself. This thesis asks whether calibration can be incorporated directly into neural network training, and how doing so affects predictive performance, posterior estimation, and uncertainty quantification.

Objectives

  • Investigate end-to-end calibration methods that put calibration objectives into neural network training.
  • Study the relationship between predictive performance, confidence, and reliability.
  • Evaluate the behavior of calibrated models under distribution shift.
  • Explore Transformer-based gene regulatory network (GRN) inference as a biomedical application.

Approach

I augmented empirical risk minimization with a differentiable calibration penalty, instead of relying only on post-hoc correction:

\[ \min_{\theta \in \Theta}\; (1-\lambda)\,\widehat{C}(f_{\theta}) + \lambda\,\widehat{E}(f_{\theta}) \]

The work combines theoretical analysis with controlled computational experiments. These examined calibration-refinement trade-offs, model capacity, objective weights, post-hoc calibration baselines, optimization-related ablations, and a covariate-shift setting in which the input distribution changes while the conditional label mechanism stays fixed. The GRN study used Transformer-based edge prediction on DREAM5 and SERGIO data and evaluated ranking, probability-sensitive, and calibration metrics.

Selected findings

Across 30 random seeds in the controlled one-dimensional setting, λ = 0.1 reduced the posterior mean-squared error at capacities m = 10, 100, and 1000 from 0.0189, 0.0482, and 0.0378 to 0.0101, 0.0127, and 0.0129, and the expected calibration error also decreased at all three capacities. The covariate-shift experiment was encouraging, but ablations showed that some of the gains were associated with effective step-size changes, and calibration-only training performed poorly.

On DREAM5 Network 1, the medium-capacity BCE model with λ = 0.5 improved AUROC from 0.857 to 0.882, AUPR from 0.787 to 0.833, and the Brier score from 0.136 to 0.120. Results were not uniform across datasets or capacities, and cross-network calibration did not transfer automatically. The thesis therefore stresses conditional effectiveness, careful evaluation, and the complementary role of post-hoc methods, rather than claiming a universal benefit.

Contribution and relevance

The work connects calibration objectives with statistical-learning questions and tests them on a biomedical prediction task. It strengthened my interest in reliable probabilistic prediction, calibration under distribution shift, and the conditions under which trustworthy-ML interventions give reproducible benefits.

Technical stack: Python, PyTorch, Statistical Learning, Neural Networks, Transformers

CoDHy: An AI Co-Scientist for Biomarker-Guided Drug Combination Hypothesis Generation

CAIMed Research Group, Peter L. Reichertz Institute for Medical Informatics & Medizinische Hochschule Hannover. Advisors: Dr. rer. nat. Zahra Ahmadi and Dr.-Ing. Raneen Younis.
Research area: Biomedical AI, LLMs, Knowledge Graphs, Scientific Reasoning, Drug Combination Research

Research motivation

Biomedical research increasingly needs evidence from molecular, genomic, clinical, and literature sources to be brought together. Large language models are promising for scientific reasoning, but their outputs can suffer from incomplete evidence, inconsistent information, and overconfident conclusions. This project develops an AI co-scientist that supports biomarker-guided drug combination hypothesis generation through evidence-based reasoning.

Objectives

  • Develop a multi-agent system for biomedical hypothesis generation.
  • Integrate heterogeneous biomedical evidence through Knowledge Graph-aware Retrieval-Augmented Generation.
  • Support reasoning over molecular, genomic, clinical, and literature information.
  • Investigate calibrated confidence and risk-controlled mechanisms for scientific reasoning under incomplete evidence.

Approach

CoDHy (Combination-Drug Hypothesis Explorer) is an interactive AI co-scientist. It builds task-specific knowledge graphs from biomedical literature and curated resources, and combines relation-aware graph embeddings with agent-based reasoning to generate, validate, and rank evidence-grounded drug-combination hypotheses. The workflow includes consistency checks, evidence-based validation, iterative feedback, ranking, and a persistent “taboo” memory for rejected combinations. A web interface lets researchers specify a biomarker, a cancer context, and the literature scope, and then inspect the evidence and intermediate results.

The system combines large language models, Knowledge Graphs, and Retrieval-Augmented Generation in a multi-agent architecture. The focus is on evidence-grounded reasoning and on mechanisms that account for uncertainty and for the limits of the available evidence.

Contribution and outcome

I am an equal-contribution co-author and contribute to developing the LLM- and Knowledge Graph-aware system. It is designed to support researcher-led exploration and prioritization, not to present generated combinations as clinically validated treatments. The work was accepted at IEEE ICDM 2026 (Demo Track) and will appear in the workshop proceedings, with the paper “From literature to hypotheses: An AI co-scientist system for biomarker-guided drug combination hypothesis generation”.

Technical stack: LLMs, RAG, Knowledge Graphs, Multi-Agent Systems, Python

Independent research

Grokking and Normal Hyperbolicity of the Interpolation Manifold

Accepted at SKILL 2026, German Informatics Society (oral presentation).
Research area: Theoretical Machine Learning, Neural Network Generalization, Dynamical Systems

Research motivation

Grokking is a phenomenon in which neural networks show a delayed transition from memorization to generalization during training. Understanding the mathematical mechanism behind this transition is an open problem in theoretical machine learning. This project asks whether the sharp generalization transition can be interpreted as a geometric change in the interpolation manifold.

Objectives

  • Investigate the relationship between grokking and the geometry of the interpolation manifold.
  • Explore whether the transition can be interpreted as a loss of normal hyperbolicity.
  • Develop and evaluate a diagnostic for studying this geometric interpretation.

Approach

I formulated an optimizer-agnostic diagnostic based on the smallest nonzero singular value of the residual Jacobian. For squared loss, this quantity corresponds to the slowest normal restoring rate of the interpolation manifold. I evaluated it in a two-layer ReLU network trained on modular addition, tracking the diagnostic alongside training and test accuracy and examining the six smallest singular values across five initialization seeds.

Findings and limitations

The diagnostic did not collapse during the grokking transition. It was near zero before memorization and reached its largest values during the transition. The six smallest singular values showed no subspace-local collapse, and the pattern held across five seeds. This is preliminary evidence against a global loss of normal hyperbolicity in the studied setting and favors a smooth-contraction interpretation.

Contribution and outcome

The project connects an empirical phenomenon in neural network training with concepts from dynamical systems and differential geometry. It resulted in the paper “Is grokking a loss of normal hyperbolicity of the interpolation manifold?”, accepted for oral presentation at SKILL 2026, the student conference of the German Informatics Society, and to appear in GI-Edition Lecture Notes in Informatics.

Technical stack: Python, PyTorch, Neural Networks, Numerical Linear Algebra

Independent research

An Ontology-Driven Biomedical Knowledge Graph for Drug Link Prediction

Knowledge-graph engineering and leakage-controlled link prediction
Research area: Biomedical Knowledge Graphs, Semantic Web, Graph Machine Learning, Computational Pharmacology

Research motivation

Biomedical evidence on drugs is spread across registries, regulatory databases, experimental screens, and the literature, each with its own schema and identifiers. Combining them into one queryable, validated resource is a prerequisite for evidence-grounded computational drug research. It is also a place where leakage and shortcuts can make graph-ML link-prediction results look better than they are. This project builds such a resource from real public data and tests whether it can support drug-condition, drug-sensitivity, and drug-adverse-event link prediction.

Objectives

  • Build an ontology-driven knowledge graph that integrates ClinicalTrials.gov, openFDA, GDSC2, and PubMed.
  • Enforce data quality with SHACL validation and add rule-based inference with OWL-RL.
  • Support competency questions in SPARQL, including cases where reasoning changes the answer.
  • Evaluate link prediction under explicit leakage controls and against a popularity baseline.

Approach

I designed an OWL 2 ontology (15 classes, 31 object properties, 24 datatype properties) and mapped SQLite views to RDF with R2RML (morph-kgc), using one named graph per source. The pipeline covers fetching, entity resolution, and LLM-based relation extraction from PubMed abstracts. An extracted relation is kept only if its evidence sentence appears verbatim in the abstract, and every assertion keeps its provenance.

Validation uses SHACL and reasoning uses OWL-RL. Ten SPARQL competency questions are included, two of them run with and without inference. For graph ML, I implemented link prediction with PyKEEN (TransE, RotatE, ComplEx) and an R-GCN with DistMult, together with a popularity baseline. The tasks include a temporal split for drug-condition prediction. Leakage controls include removing trial nodes, excluding inverse relations, time-aware context edges, and a hard no-leakage assertion before training. A test checks that the RDF graph and the ML graph contain identical edges.

Findings and limitations

Ten stages are verified end to end by 75 offline tests on hand-written fixtures. These include checks that SPARQL answers match an independent SQL/Python computation, that SHACL conforms on the output and fails on deliberately bad data, and that the evaluator matches a brute-force implementation and PyKEEN’s own evaluator.

Connectors to the live APIs, the quality of live LLM extraction, and runtime and ML results are not yet verified. Entity resolution is rule-based, and the default seed set is a sample of 60 drugs.

Contribution and outcome

The project is a reproducible, test-covered pipeline from public biomedical data to a validated knowledge graph and a leakage-controlled link-prediction benchmark, and it is explicit about what is verified and what is not. It extends my GDSC2-based drug-response work and my knowledge-graph work on CoDHy.

Technical stack: Python, RDF/OWL, R2RML, SHACL, SPARQL, PyKEEN, PyTorch Geometric

Independent research

Evaluating Grounding and Abstention in GraphRAG over a Pharmacogenomics Knowledge Graph

Evaluation harness for grounded question answering
Research area: Trustworthy AI, GraphRAG, Knowledge Graphs, LLM Evaluation, Pharmacogenomics

Research motivation

Language models that answer questions over scientific knowledge can produce fluent but unsupported statements. For cell-line pharmacogenomics, it matters that every claim traces back to graph evidence, that measured and predicted drug responses are not mixed up, and that the system declines to answer when the graph has no answer. This project asks how different retrieval strategies compare on grounding, abstention, and citation validity.

Objectives

  • Compare retrieval strategies for question answering over a pharmacogenomics knowledge graph.
  • Measure whether answers are supported by cited graph evidence, not only whether they are correct.
  • Evaluate when a system should abstain on unanswerable questions.
  • Keep measured and predicted drug responses distinct and labelled in the evidence.

Approach

I implemented four strategies on the same questions: vector RAG (a TF-IDF baseline), text-to-SPARQL (schema-grounded generation with validation and retry), k-hop neighborhood expansion after entity linking, and a hybrid of linking, text-to-SPARQL, and neighborhood fallback. Answers are structured JSON (answer, citations, abstain, note).

Generated SPARQL is restricted to SELECT, has SERVICE calls stripped, and gets a row limit. The evaluation harness generates questions from templates (single-hop, reverse-hop, two-hop, ranking, filtered, predicted, and unanswerable) and computes gold answers by SPARQL on the graph. Metrics include precision, recall and F1, correct and excessive abstention, citation validity, unsupported-answer rate, retrieval and linking recall, SPARQL validity, and latency.

Findings and limitations

The repository contributes the evaluation harness and does not report benchmark results. The stated limitations: the unsupported-answer check is a string match rather than a semantic entailment check, questions are template-based and paraphrase robustness is not measured, and the in-memory RDF backend cannot enforce query timeouts. The data is cell-line research data, not clinical decision support.

Contribution and outcome

The project is a reusable harness for testing whether GraphRAG answers are grounded, cited correctly, and abstained on when appropriate. It ties my trustworthy-ML interests (reliability, abstention, honest evaluation) to knowledge-graph question answering in a biomedical setting.

Technical stack: Python, GraphRAG, SPARQL, RDF, LLM evaluation, FastAPI, Docker

Independent research

Uncertainty Quantification in Neural Networks

Neural networks, Gaussian processes and distribution shift
Research area: Uncertainty Quantification, Gaussian Processes, Deep Learning, Distribution Shift

Research motivation

Reliable uncertainty estimation is essential when machine learning models are applied to scientific problems where predictions may be made under conditions that differ from the training distribution. This project investigates the relationship between finite-width neural networks and their infinite-width Gaussian Process limits, with a focus on uncertainty estimates under distribution shift.

Objectives

  • Investigate uncertainty estimation through the connection between neural networks and Gaussian Processes.
  • Compare uncertainty estimates from different modeling approaches.
  • Evaluate empirical behavior under distribution shift.
  • Examine the strengths and limitations of the different uncertainty estimation methods.

Approach

I compared Neural Network Gaussian Processes (NNGPs), deep ensembles, Monte Carlo dropout, and RBF Gaussian processes, focusing on their empirical uncertainty behavior under distribution shift. The work is a comparative study of probabilistic and deep-learning approaches.

Contribution and outcome

The project connects theoretical perspectives on neural network limits with practical uncertainty estimation methods. It produced a comparative investigation of uncertainty estimation methods and their empirical behavior under distribution shift, and strengthened my understanding of how neural-network limits relate to practical uncertainty estimation.

Technical stack: Python, Gaussian Processes, Neural Networks, Statistical Learning

Independent research

SupplierGraph: A Knowledge Graph for German and EU Public Procurement and Supplier Risk

End-to-end knowledge graph on open procurement data
Research area: Knowledge Graphs, Entity Resolution, Knowledge-Graph Embeddings, GraphRAG

I built an end-to-end knowledge graph for German and EU public procurement and supplier risk on real open data (GLEIF, OCDS, CPV, OpenSanctions, EU Directive 2014/24). The pipeline covers OWL 2 ontology design, R2RML integration, entity resolution, SHACL validation, OWL RL and SPARQL rule reasoning, TransE/RotatE supplier recommendation, and GraphRAG question answering.

On a sample of 20,000 tenders, the graph holds 400,532 triples with 0 SHACL violations. I evaluated each component against baselines and report the weak results as they are: the recommender is barely above chance on new buyer-supplier pairs (MRR 0.007), and an 8B-model GraphRAG reached an answer recall of only 0.091 in its first run on 14 competency questions.

The project gave me hands-on experience with the full knowledge-graph lifecycle and with honest evaluation, including reporting negative results and the limits of a pipeline.
