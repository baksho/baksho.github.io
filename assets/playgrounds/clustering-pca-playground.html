Clustering and PCA Playground

Two classic ways to find structure in unlabelled data. Clustering groups 2D points: watch k-means and a Gaussian mixture model (GMM) go through their steps one at a time, with the mixture fitted by EM, by variational inference or by Gibbs sampling. PCA squashes a 3D point cloud onto a flat plane: tilt the plane yourself and compare it with the plane that PCA picks. Choose the topic at the top of the Settings panel on the right.

How it works

What this page is, and is not. Every data set here is made up by the page: random points drawn from a few simple shapes. The algorithms are exact and small, but real data is rarely this tidy, and the pictures only show 2D and 3D. The numbers quoted below come from my own runs of this code.

Clustering

Clustering means splitting unlabelled points into groups of points that are close to each other. Both methods here need you to choose the number of clusters K and both start from K randomly chosen data points, so a different start can end in a different answer. Each method repeats two half-steps until nothing changes any more. The Step button does one half-step, so you can see both.

k-means

  • Assign: every point joins the cluster with the nearest centre.
  • Update: every centre moves to the mean of the points that joined it. A centre that has no points keeps its old place.

The quantity k-means lowers is the sum of the squared distances from each point to its centre (inertia). Neither half-step can raise it, so it falls until the assignments stop changing (Lloyd's algorithm; see Lloyd 1982). It stops at a local minimum, not necessarily the best one. On "Three round blobs" with K = 3, I ran 8 different initial centres: 3 of the 8 ended more than 1% above the best inertia found. The shaded regions are the cells of nearest-centre territory, which are straight-edged, so k-means is best at compact, roundish groups of similar size.

Gaussian mixture and EM

A Gaussian mixture says the data came from K bell-shaped clouds. Each cloud has a weight (how many points it explains), a centre and a covariance (how wide it is and in which direction it is stretched). The ellipses show one and two standard deviations. EM (expectation-maximisation) fits these by repeating two half-steps:

  • E-step: for each point, compute the probability that each cloud produced it (its responsibilities, which add up to 1). Dots are coloured by blending the cluster colours with these probabilities.
  • M-step: re-estimate every cloud's weight, centre and covariance from the points, with each point counted in proportion to its responsibility.
rik = wk N(xi | μk, Σk) / Σj wj N(xi | μj, Σj)
μk = Σi rik xi / Σi rik,   Σk = Σi rik (xi − μk)(xi − μk)T / Σi rik,   wk = Σi rik / n

The chart shows the average log-likelihood of the data under the current mixture, which EM tries to raise. In theory it never decreases (Dempster, Laird and Rubin 1977). This page adds 0.001 to the diagonal of every covariance so that a cloud cannot shrink onto a few points. That small floor means the plain log-likelihood can dip very slightly: in 192 runs of mine (4 data sets × K = 1 to 6 × 8 initial centres, 150 EM iterations each), 49 showed at least one dip, the largest about 9×10−6 in the average log-likelihood, far too small to see on the chart. The page stops when the log-likelihood changes by less than 10−6 in one iteration.

k-means can be seen as a stripped-down relative of this: if every cloud is forced to be round and equally narrow, and the narrowness goes to zero, the responsibilities become hard 0-or-1 assignments (Bishop 2006, chapter 9). The extra freedom of the mixture costs more iterations: on the four data sets here, 8 runs of mine took 2 to 19 k-means iterations but 9 to 217 EM iterations.

Two Bayesian ways to fit the same mixture: variational inference and Gibbs sampling

EM returns one answer: the weights, centres and covariances that make the data most likely. The two methods below are Bayesian. They put a prior on those parameters and ask for the posterior: the whole range of parameter values that are plausible given the data. Both use the same prior, which is my own choice and not a standard one. The weights get a Dirichlet prior whose concentration α0 is the slider in the Settings panel. Each cluster's centre and precision (inverse covariance) get a normal–Wishart prior centred on the average of the data, with a guess for a cluster's variance of a quarter of the data's average variance per axis, and a strength of 1 point for the centre and 2 for the covariance, so the data dominate it.

Variational inference (VI). The exact posterior over assignments and parameters is hard to compute. VI instead looks for the closest distribution of a simpler form, in which the assignments are independent of the parameters (mean-field), with closeness measured by the KL divergence. Maximising the evidence lower bound (ELBO) is the same as minimising that divergence (Blei, Kucukelbir and McAuliffe 2017; Bishop 2006, section 10.2). The two half-steps look like EM: update the responsibilities, using averages over the uncertainty in each cluster's parameters, then update each cluster's parameter distribution from the points it claims plus the prior. The ellipses show the average centre and the inverse of the average precision; the chart shows the ELBO divided by the number of points.

Gibbs sampling. Instead of approximating the posterior, draw random samples from it. One iteration first draws every point's cluster at random given the current parameters, then draws new weights, centres and covariances at random given those assignments. Both draws come from exact conditional distributions of this model, so after enough iterations the draws follow the posterior (Geman and Geman 1984 introduced Gibbs sampling; Diebolt and Robert 1994 applied it to mixtures). What you see is one random mixture per iteration, so it wobbles. The faint dots show where each centre was in the last 40 draws, and the chart shows the average log-likelihood of the current draw, which fluctuates around a level instead of converging. This page simply stops after 500 iterations and runs one chain from one random start, with no burn-in removed and no convergence test.

What the Bayesian methods add. With a small α0 they can leave surplus clusters empty, so K acts as an upper limit. A cluster counts as “in use” when it holds at least one point (VI: one point's worth of weight); unused clusters are not drawn. On “Three round blobs” (240 points) with K = 6 and K = 10, VI with α0 = 0.1 ended with exactly 3 clusters in use in all 8 starts (ARI 1.00), whereas EM, which must use all K, had a mean ARI of 0.72 for K = 6 and 0.46 for K = 10. Gibbs sampling with α0 = 0.01 and K = 6 also ended with 3 clusters in use in all 8 runs (mean ARI 0.98, lowest 0.95). With α0 = 10, neither method pruned anything in my runs: VI used all 6 (ARI 0.80) or all 10 clusters (0.65), and Gibbs all 6 (0.67). VI results for α0 from 0.001 to 1 were nearly the same. The pruning depends on this prior and these data: on “Overlapping blobs” with K = 6, VI kept 3 or 4 clusters in use although the data came from 3 groups.

Limits of the Bayesian methods. VI is still a local method: with K = 3 on the three blobs, 2 of my 8 starts ended with only 2 clusters in use, having merged two groups, so press Restart. Mean-field VI is known to understate uncertainty; on the well-separated blobs the Gibbs spread of the centres was about 0.08 to 0.09 and VI's implied spread about 0.08, so the difference was small there, but I did not test harder cases. Gibbs sampling is slower and needs many iterations; its average log-likelihood sat at about −3.24 on the blobs, about 0.05 below the best EM value (−3.198 at K = 3), which is expected, because a typical posterior draw is not the likelihood maximum. In principle the colours of the clusters can swap between draws (label switching); I did not look for it. The numbers behind these statements come from 8 starts per setting.

How I checked them. The ELBO never decreased in 192 runs of 80 iterations (4 data sets, K = 2, 3, 6, 10, four values of α0, 3 starts); the worst change was −2×10−12, i.e. rounding. For three fitted states, the ELBO computed from the formulas agreed with a separate Monte-Carlo estimate in Python (scipy) to within 0.02 (values near −870, −834 and −749). The fitted means and covariances matched scikit-learn's BayesianGaussianMixture with the same priors, means to 3×10−7 and covariances to about 10−6, when my run was started from the same optimum. For the sampler, the Gamma and Wishart draws reproduced their exact expectations, and a Geweke “getting it right” test (alternating the sampler with fresh data simulated from the model) reproduced the prior for the weights, centres, covariances and precisions; and on the blobs the Gibbs posterior means of the centres agreed with VI to within 0.012.

What to try

To compare methods fairly I used the adjusted Rand index (ARI), which is 1 when the clusters match the true groups the page generated and about 0 for a random split. All numbers below are for 240 points, the suggested K, and 8 different initial centres.

  • Tilted elongated clusters. k-means cuts straight between nearby centres and keeps getting this wrong (ARI 0.61 to 0.64). The mixture can stretch its ellipses along the groups and reached 0.87 in all 8 runs.
  • Three round blobs. Both do well from a good start. k-means had a mean ARI of 0.79 (range 0.44 to 1.00) and the mixture 0.93 (range 0.45 to 1.00); the bad starts are the local minima, so press “Restart with different initial centres” a few times.
  • Overlapping blobs. The true groups overlap, so no method can recover them cleanly: ARI 0.50 to 0.59 for k-means and 0.24 to 0.47 for the mixture. A low score here is a property of the data, not a bug.
  • Two moons. Both methods assume blob-like clusters and fail on curved ones: k-means scored 0.30 and the mixture 0.25 to 0.54.
  • Too many or too few clusters. Set K to 1 or 10 on three blobs with k-means or EM. Nothing tells the algorithm that K is wrong: the best sum of squared distances over 8 starts falls from 1810 (K = 1) to 86 (K = 10), and the best average log-likelihood of EM rises from −3.198 (K = 3) to −2.994 (K = 10).
  • Let the data choose K. Set K = 10 on three blobs, pick variational inference or Gibbs sampling, and watch clusters drop out (the counter in the Progress panel). Then move α0 to 10 and restart: all clusters stay in use.
  • See the uncertainty. With Gibbs sampling, lower the number of points to 60 and watch the faint dots: the centres move around much more than with 1000 points.

PCA on a 3D cloud

Principal component analysis (PCA) finds the directions in which a cloud of points varies most. It centres the cloud, computes the 3×3 covariance matrix, and takes its eigenvectors: the first principal axis (PC1) is the direction of largest variance, PC2 is the largest among the directions at right angles to PC1, and PC3 is what remains. Each eigenvalue is the variance along its axis. The sheet in the 3D view is a plane through the centre of the cloud; the right-hand panel shows every point as seen on that plane.

Projecting onto a plane keeps part of the variance and loses the rest. For a plane with orthonormal directions u and v, the variance kept is uTCu + vTCv, where C is the covariance matrix; the lost variance is the total minus this, and it equals the average squared distance of the points from the plane (up to the factor n/(n−1)). So the plane that keeps the most variance is also the plane that lies closest to the points in the least-squares sense (Pearson 1901). That best plane is the one spanned by PC1 and PC2. The “Snap” button moves the plane there, and the bars show how much your plane keeps compared with it.

I checked the covariance and eigenvectors against numpy, and the formula for the kept variance against a direct computation on the projected points. In 2000 random planes, none kept more than the PC1–PC2 plane. Every random sample is built from mirror-image pairs of points about the centre of the cloud, so the cloud is exactly balanced about its centre in every view.

What to try

  • Flat disc, tilted. Most of the variance lies in a plane that is not aligned with the screen. Rotate the plane by hand until the bar is near the tick, then snap to see how close you got.
  • Round ball. All directions have nearly the same variance (about 43%, 32% and 24% for the default sample), so no plane is much better than any other: the best plane keeps 76% of the variance, while a randomly chosen plane keeps two thirds on average. The order of the principal axes is largely chance.
  • Cigar. One axis carries about 87% of the variance (default sample), so any plane that contains the long axis keeps at least that much.
  • Two groups split along a thin direction. The two colours differ only along PC3, which has little variance. In the default sample the three axes carry 60%, 28% and 12% of the variance, and the groups' centres differ by 1.8 along PC3 but only by 0.6 along PC1 and 0.2 along PC2, so the best plane for variance squeezes the two groups on top of each other. PCA never looks at the colours: variance and usefulness for telling groups apart are different things. Tilt the plane so that it contains PC3 and the groups separate again.

Limits

  • k-means and EM need K in advance and depend on the starting centres. Neither is guaranteed to find the best solution. The Bayesian methods treat K as an upper limit, but their priors are my own choices and the results depend on them; I only varied α0.
  • The mixture here uses full covariances and one fixed way to start (random data points as centres, round clouds of equal size). Other ways to start can give other answers.
  • PCA only finds straight, linear directions. It cannot unroll a curved shape, and it is not told about groups or labels.
  • The data are small, made-up samples, and the numbers in the text vary with the sample.
