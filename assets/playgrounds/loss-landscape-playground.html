Loss Landscape Explorer

A small neural network is trained on a 2-D toy problem, and then its training loss is evaluated on a plane through weight space: the loss at θ + α·d₁ + β·d₂ for two random directions d₁, d₂ and a grid of α, β. Look at it as a contour map or a 3-D surface. Change the width or depth, scrub through training epochs, compare a second model side by side, and put numbers on "sharp" and "flat": the largest Hessian eigenvalue and the loss increase under random weight perturbations. The page also shows where this picture is misleading: the same function can be made to look arbitrarily sharp by rescaling its weights.

How it works

What is plotted

A network with n weights is one point θ in an n-dimensional space, and its training loss is a function L(θ) of that point. You cannot look at n dimensions, so the page cuts a flat two-dimensional slice through the trained weights, spanned by two random directions, and evaluates the loss on a grid of that slice:

f(α, β) = L(θ + α·d₁ + β·d₂), α, β ∈ [−R, R]

The contour map and the 3-D surface are two views of the same grid (21×21 to 41×41 evaluations of the loss on the training set). The ring in the middle is the trained network itself. The epoch slider re-centres the slice on the weights saved at that epoch, so scrubbing it shows the basin being carved out while Adam or SGD runs. The loss drawn is the plain cross-entropy on the training points; a weight-decay term, if you switch it on, is not part of it.

The network and the training

A multilayer perceptron with 2 inputs, depth hidden layers of width units (tanh or ReLU), and one output logit with a sigmoid and cross-entropy loss, on a 2-D toy problem (two moons, circles, XOR blobs or a spiral; the training set has 20 to 150 points, the test set always 400). Initialisation is Glorot (tanh) or He (ReLU); training runs for 1000 epochs with Adam or SGD with momentum 0.9, full batch or mini-batches, optional weight decay, and optionally sharpness-aware minimisation (SAM): each step first moves the weights a distance ρ uphill along the gradient and then uses the gradient found there. Everything runs in your browser; the gradient was checked against finite differences and the loss, gradient and Hessian eigenvalue against an independent NumPy implementation (the eigenvalue agreed to about 1e-8 relative on the network I tried).

Why the directions are "filter-normalised"

If the two directions were plain Gaussian noise, a step of size 1 would mean very different things for a neuron with large weights and one with small weights, and two networks with differently scaled weights could not be compared at all. Li et al. therefore rescale each direction so that, for every neuron, the part that perturbs its incoming weights has the same norm as that neuron's own weight vector; biases are left alone. A coordinate of 1 then means "perturb every neuron by about its own size". The option raw Gaussian skips this (it only rescales the whole direction to the norm of θ) so you can see the difference.

The two numbers for "sharp"

  • λ_max, the largest eigenvalue of the Hessian of the training loss: the curvature along the steepest direction. It is found by power iteration on Hessian-vector products (central differences of the analytic gradient). It is measured in the raw weight coordinates. The finite-difference step has to be tiny (1e-8): on ReLU networks a larger step crosses activation kinks, and with the step I first used (1e-4) the result was 10 to 30 times too large on the trained networks I checked. With the small step it matched the exact Gauss–Newton eigenvalue on the two ReLU networks I compared (for example 0.0114 against 0.0114, and 0.0024 against 0.0024); I did not test more networks than that.
  • Mean loss increase at r = 0.25: take 24 random filter-normalised directions, move the weights by 0.25 of their own size along each, and average how much the training loss goes up. This is the kind of quantity the flat-minimum literature uses (a neighbourhood in which the loss stays low). The chart "Loss vs perturbation size" shows the same thing for a range of r.

The table also shows a Hutchinson estimate of the trace of the Hessian (the sum of all its eigenvalues, from 16 random ±1 probes). It is a noisy estimate, and it can come out negative if the weights are not at a minimum.

A random direction is a poor probe of the sharpest direction: for an isotropic random direction the expected curvature is the trace of the Hessian divided by n, far smaller than λ_max when a few eigenvalues dominate. That is one reason a random-plane picture can look gentle while λ_max is large, and why the page reports both.

What I measured

All numbers below are medians over 24 runs (new data and new initialisation each time) on noisy two moons (noise 0.30, 60 training points, 1000 epochs, Adam with learning rate 0.02 unless stated). I measured them in the same code the page runs; they describe this toy problem and these recipes, nothing more. The seed-to-seed spread is large, so a single run, or the single run each scenario shows, can differ from the median. After 1000 epochs most of these networks fit the training set almost perfectly (median training loss 1e-4 or below for Adam), which matters for how the numbers read; see "Training length" below.

Width. Both numbers fall as the network gets wider, for tanh and for ReLU. Paired by seed, the width-48 network had the smaller loss increase than the width-4 network in 24 of 24 tanh runs and 22 of 24 ReLU runs, and the smaller λ_max in 21 and 23 of 24. Depth. Going from one hidden layer to four (width 16, tanh): the loss increase was higher for the deeper network in 21 of 24 paired runs (median ratio 2.1), but λ_max was lower in 13 of 24 (median ratio 0.78), so here the two numbers do not agree. Recipes. The two sharpness numbers do not always agree. The paired counts below come from the scenario experiment (the same seeds the page's scenarios use) and can differ by one from the table, which is a separate 24-run set.

  • SGD with batch 8 against SGD with full batch: λ_max was lower in all 24 paired runs (median ratio 0.18), which is the direction Keskar et al. report for small batches. The loss increase at r = 0.25 was higher in 18 of 24 runs (median ratio 1.37). The test accuracy was higher for batch 8 in only 5 of 24 runs. This is not a clean batch-size experiment: the two recipes also differ in learning rate (0.05 and 0.02) and in the number of steps (about 1,000 against about eight times as many).
  • SAM against plain Adam: the loss increase was lower in 22 of 24 runs (median ratio 0.56), but λ_max was lower in only 6 of 24 (median ratio 2.1). Test accuracy was higher in 15 of 24 runs (median 93.2 % against 92.9 %); I did not test whether that is more than chance.
  • Weight decay against plain Adam: the loss increase was lower in 22 of 24 runs (median ratio 0.34), while λ_max was lower in only 1 of 24 (median ratio 36). Test accuracy was higher in 18 of 24 (93.8 % against 92.9 %).

Note that the recipes end at different training losses (table above). The weight-decay runs sit at a training loss of about 0.05 while plain Adam is near zero, so the comparison mixes sharpness with how far each fit got; I did not separate those two.

As the training loss goes to zero, λ_max keeps falling (the curvature of a cross-entropy loss shrinks as the predictions become confident), while the loss increase along random directions rises and the test loss rises with it; the test accuracy stays about the same. So the same network looks flatter or sharper depending on which number and which epoch you look at, and sharpness values are only comparable between networks trained to a similar loss. (This table used a different draw of the 24 random directions from the width table, hence 0.181 against 0.198.)

Does sharpness predict generalisation here? Over the 264 run entries of these experiments (24 of them are repeats, because the width-16 Adam runs appear in two tables), the rank correlation between test accuracy and λ_max was 0.02 and with the loss increase −0.14; against the test loss it was −0.23 for λ_max and 0.30 for the loss increase. All of these are weak. The loss increase has the sign a "flatter is better" story predicts for the test loss (a larger increase goes with a higher test loss), while λ_max has the opposite sign for it. Within the 24 width-16 tanh runs alone the correlations with test accuracy were −0.28 (λ_max) and 0.14 (loss increase). On this small problem I find no useful relationship; Li et al. report a correlation for filter-normalised sharpness on much larger networks, and I make no claim about those.

Rescaling a ReLU network changes its sharpness but not its function. For a ReLU network, multiplying the first layer's weights and biases by a and dividing the next layer's weights by a leaves the output exactly the same, so the loss is the same too. The "ReLU rescaling" slider does this. Dinh et al. use this symmetry to argue that sharpness measured in raw weight coordinates cannot explain generalisation, because it can be made as large as you like without changing the function. On this page, for 8 trained 16×2 ReLU networks (median): a = 1, 2, 4, 8 give a raw λ_max of 0.041, 0.055, 0.142, 0.513, while the loss increase with filter-normalised directions stays at 0.3063 for every a (the landscape itself is identical to rounding error in my test). Across 12 runs (seeds 1 to 12), λ_max at a = 8 was larger than at a = 1 in all 12, by a median factor of about 12 (range 2 to 23). With raw Gaussian directions the landscape changes visibly as well; with filter-normalised ones it does not. Filter normalisation removes this particular symmetry, not every possible one.

Caveats

  • A 2-D slice with random directions shows the loss along two directions out of n. Another pair of directions would give another picture (use "Draw new random directions" to see how much it varies).
  • The problem is tiny (2-D inputs, at most about 7,000 weights), the loss is cross-entropy, and the network is trained for a fixed 1000 epochs. Most Adam runs reach a training loss near zero, where sharpness numbers keep changing with training length (see above). Results here need not carry over to image or language models.
  • The sweep charts train 8 runs per setting (new data each time) and show their median and range. λ_max has a heavy tail, so with so few runs the medians are noisy, and the 24-run medians above are the more reliable numbers; at some settings the two can visibly disagree. A sweep of 8 runs per setting at 1000 epochs also takes a while in the browser.
  • λ_max and the trace use finite-difference Hessian-vector products with a step of 1e-8; at a ReLU network's kinks the Hessian is not defined, and the numbers are those of the function away from kinks. I verified the step against the exact eigenvalue on only a few ReLU networks.
