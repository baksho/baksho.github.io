Bias–Variance Playground

Fit a model to a small noisy sample, then draw a fresh sample and fit again, many times over. The spread of the fitted curves is the model's variance, the gap between their average and the truth is its bias, and together with the noise they add up to the error you can expect on new data.

What is happening here?

A model is trained on a random sample, so the model itself is random: a different sample gives a different curve. Pick a point x and let f(x) be the true value, f̂(x) the fitted value, and σ² the variance of the noise in the data. With the expectation taken over training samples and over new noise, the expected squared error at x splits into three parts:

E[(y − f̂(x))²] = (E[f̂(x)] − f(x))² + Var[f̂(x)] + σ²

Bias

The gap between the average fitted curve (orange) and the truth (dark line). A model that is too simple to follow the truth is wrong in the same way on every sample, and that is bias.

Variance

How much the fitted curve changes from sample to sample (the spread of the blue curves). A very flexible model chases the noise in each sample, so its curves scatter.

Noise

The part no model can remove, because it is in the data. It sets the floor under the test error.

The models

  • Polynomial: least squares in a Legendre polynomial basis on [−1, 1], with an optional ridge penalty of strength λn on every coefficient except the constant. Higher degree means a more flexible model.
  • k-nearest neighbours: the prediction at x is the average of the k training targets closest to x. Small k is flexible, k = n predicts the overall mean.
  • Kernel smoother: a Nadaraya–Watson average with Gaussian weights of bandwidth h. A small bandwidth is flexible.

How the numbers are computed

The page keeps K training samples. At each x on a grid of 121 points it averages the K predictions and measures their spread (the unbiased sample variance). The squared gap between the average and the truth overstates bias² by about Var/K, because the average itself is noisy, so that amount is subtracted. Both are then averaged over the grid, which treats test points as uniform on [0, 1]. These are estimates from K samples, so with few fits they are noisy; use more fits to steady them. The “measured” test error is the average squared error against fresh noisy targets and should land close to the sum of the three parts. The curve below the plot repeats the calculation for every flexibility setting, using up to 50 of the samples.

Things to try

  • Fit the sine wave with degree 0, 1, 3 and 9 and watch the average curve and the spread change.
  • Hold the model fixed and increase the noise or reduce the training points. Which of the two parts grows?
  • On the step function, compare a polynomial with k-NN. Where does each one lose?
  • On the Runge function, raise the polynomial degree and look at the bias and variance strip near x = 0 and x = 1.
  • Switch to a fixed grid of x positions. Only the noise changes between samples now.
  • Add a little ridge penalty to a high-degree polynomial and see which of the two parts it reduces.
  • Press play to keep drawing fresh samples and watch the curves flicker.
