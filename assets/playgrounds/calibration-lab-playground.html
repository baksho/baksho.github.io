Calibration Lab

When a classifier says “80% sure”, is it right about 80% of the time? That is calibration. A model can have high accuracy and still be badly calibrated, and a calibrated model can be a poor one. This page lets you diagnose calibration with a reliability diagram, fix it with three standard recalibration methods, see how it breaks under distribution shift, and play a forecasting game that shows why honest probabilities are the best policy. All data are synthetic, so the page knows the true probability of every point. Choose the mode at the top of the Settings panel on the right.

How you are scored. Each round you announce a probability f for class 1 and the class y (1 or 0) is then drawn. The Brier score of the round is (f − y)²; the log loss is −log f if y = 1 and −log(1 − f) if y = 0. Lower is better for both. Both are proper scoring rules: your expected score is lowest when you announce the probability you really believe, so there is nothing to gain by exaggerating or hedging (see the “Cost of misreporting” chart). How it works

What this page is, and is not. Every data set is made up by the page: points in the plane drawn from known mixtures of Gaussian blobs, so the page knows the exact probability that each point is class 1. That is what lets it show a perfect reference, but it is a toy: real data have no known probabilities, more dimensions, and models far more complex than a polynomial logistic regression. I wrote the model, the metrics and the recalibrators myself and checked them against independent code (below). Every number quoted here comes from my own runs of this code on made-up data, averaged over random samples where a ± is given, and will differ a little from what you see for one sample.

What calibration means

A classifier that outputs probabilities is calibrated if, among the cases where it says 70%, about 70% turn out to be class 1. Guo et al. (2017) define it for the predicted class: the probability that the predicted class is right, given that the model announced confidence p, should equal p. Calibration is a different property from accuracy. A predictor that always says “the share of class 1 in the training data” is calibrated almost by construction and useless for telling cases apart: on the default data it has about chance-level accuracy (48% on the default sample; choose Base rate only in Mode 1). In the other direction, a model can be right 90% of the time and say 99% every time. What you want is a predictor that is both accurate and calibrated, and the squared-error (Brier) and log-likelihood scores reward both together.

The page offers two conventions for the reliability diagram. Top label uses, for every point, the confidence max(p, 1−p) in the class the model predicts (class 1 if p ≥ 0.5) and records whether that prediction was right; the confidence therefore lives between 50% and 100%. P(class 1) uses p itself and records whether class 1 occurred. Both are evaluated by the same code: each test point becomes a pair (confidence, outcome), and the bins, ECE, MCE, recalibration and plots work on those pairs. The Brier score and the log-likelihood do not use the convention at all.

The data and the model

Each data set is a mixture of Gaussians: class 1 has probability π and each class is a weighted sum of Gaussian blobs. Bayes’ rule then gives the true P(class 1 | x) exactly, computed in log space for stability. The four data sets are two overlapping blobs (equal covariance, so the true boundary is a straight line), the same with only 20% class 1, XOR (two blobs per class on the diagonals) and two moons (six blobs per class along an arc). I compared the page’s probability with scipy’s multivariate normal density on 40 points per data set: the largest difference was 8×10−16.

The model is a logistic regression on polynomial features (every monomial of the two coordinates of total degree 1 to the chosen degree, each standardised using the training data). It minimises the average cross-entropy plus λ/2 times the squared size of the weights (the intercept is not penalised), by Newton’s method with a line search. The “ridge penalty” λ in Settings is that λ. Degree 1 is an ordinary logistic regression; degree 6 has 27 features and, with 100 training points, overfits. Against scikit-learn’s LogisticRegression (with C = 1/(nλ)) the logits agreed to 1.4×10−8 (degree 1, λ = 10−4), 2.5×10−6 (degree 3, λ = 0.01) and 1.1×10−7 (degree 6, λ = 1). For degree 5 with λ = 10−4 they differed by 2×10−4, but the objective values agreed to about 10−13, so both had reached the same optimum and scikit-learn’s solver had simply stopped a little early on that ill-conditioned problem.

Training, calibration and test points are drawn separately, from fixed streams of random numbers per sample, so moving a slider only adds or removes points and does not reshuffle the others. New random sample starts new streams.

Mode 1: the reliability diagram and its numbers

The test points are split into bins by confidence, either equal width or equal count (equal-count bins have sizes that differ by at most one point). For bin b with nb of n points, let confb be the mean confidence and accb the mean outcome. Then

ECE = Σb (nb/n) · |accb − confb|
MCE = maxb |accb − confb|
Brier = mean (p − y)²    NLL = mean [−y log p − (1−y) log(1−p)]

I call ECE and MCE the expected and maximum calibration error as popularised by Pakdaman Naeini, Cooper and Hauskrecht (2015) and Guo et al. (2017). Empty bins are dropped. NLL clips the probability to [10−6, 1−10−6] so that a confident mistake costs about 13.8 rather than infinity. Each whisker is the 95% Wilson interval for the true rate in that bin (Wilson 1927); against statsmodels it agreed to within 10−16 in four test cases. I checked the metrics for both conventions and both binning schemes against an independent numpy implementation: the largest difference was 6×10−16.

ECE is a diagnostic, not a loss, and it is a noisy one. It depends on the number and placement of bins, and even a perfectly calibrated predictor has a positive measured ECE, because each bin’s observed rate is a finite-sample average. Kumar, Liang and Ma (2019) discuss why such plug-in estimates are biased. The page shows this directly through the true-probability reference, which is calibrated by construction. With top-label ECE, 15 equal-width bins and 100 fresh test sets for each size, the true probability scored an ECE of 0.111 ± 0.021 at 100 test points, 0.060 ± 0.013 at 300, 0.033 ± 0.006 at 1000 and 0.015 ± 0.003 at 5000 (mean ± standard deviation). See the chart ECE noise floor vs test size. A measured ECE has to be compared with that floor, not with zero. Likewise, more bins raise the floor: for the true probability at 5000 points the ECE grew from 0.005 with 2 bins to 0.022 with 30. The overfitted degree-6, 100-point model of the first bullet below, by comparison, changed only from 0.069 to 0.076 over the same range, because its error is far larger than the noise.

What to try in Mode 1

  • Flexible models on small samples are overconfident. On the overlapping blobs (20 random training sets each, tested on 5000 fresh points, 15 bins, top-label, λ = 10−4): a degree-1 model on 100 points had ECE 0.032 ± 0.019 and mean confidence only 0.7 points above its accuracy; degree 3 on 100 points, 0.059 ± 0.025 (4.1 points overconfident); degree 6 on 100 points, 0.074 ± 0.023 (5.9 points); degree 6 on 50 points, 0.118 ± 0.038 (11.4 points) and a log-likelihood of 0.853 against 0.389 for the true probability. With 1000 training points, degree 6 was back to 0.023 ± 0.004 (0.5 points). More data, or a simpler model, cures it here.
  • Sharpening slider s. It multiplies the logit, so s = 3 pushes every probability towards 0 or 1 while leaving every prediction unchanged. Apply it to the true-probability predictor to see a perfect predictor made overconfident without changing its accuracy at all; at s < 1 it becomes underconfident.
  • Reading the diagram. Equal-count bins put the same number of points in every dot, which keeps the whiskers comparable; equal-width bins show where the confidence actually lies but leave a few bins nearly empty. Hover over a dot to see its counts and its interval.

Mode 2: fixing calibration after training

A recalibrator is a small function from the model’s output to a new probability, fitted on a separate calibration set that was used neither for training nor for testing. Three are offered, all acting on the model’s log-odds z = logit(p).

  • Temperature scaling: p = σ(βz) with one parameter β = 1/T > 0, chosen to minimise the log-likelihood on the calibration set. It is the one-parameter variant of Platt scaling described by Guo et al. (2017). The log-likelihood is convex in β, so the page finds its minimum by bisection on the derivative. Because every logit is multiplied by the same positive number, no predicted class changes and accuracy is untouched.
  • Platt scaling: p = σ(az + b), two parameters fitted by maximum likelihood (Platt 1999). Platt also suggests slightly softened targets; this page does not do that and uses plain maximum likelihood. The shift b can change predictions for points near 50%.
  • Isotonic regression: the best non-decreasing step function from p to the outcome in the squared-error sense, fitted by pool-adjacent-violators and linearly interpolated between the distinct values of p (constant outside them). It makes no assumption beyond monotonicity, but it needs more data. Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana (2005) compared Platt scaling and isotonic regression as ways to obtain good probabilities.

Against scikit-learn on the same inputs: Platt’s (a, b) matched LogisticRegression on the logit (a very large C) to 10−8; the temperature matched a numerical minimiser of the log-likelihood to 2×10−8; and isotonic regression matched IsotonicRegression (out_of_bounds = “clip”) with a largest difference of 0.

  • When the problem really is a single scale, temperature scaling solves it. Take a degree-1 model on 1000 points, which is nearly calibrated, and set the sharpening slider to s = 3 (20 random samples, 500 calibration points, 2000 test points, 10 bins). The raw ECE was 0.118; temperature scaling fitted T = 3.03 on average (it is undoing a factor of 3) and brought the ECE to 0.022, against 0.023 for the undistorted model. For s = 0.5 it found T = 0.51. Platt scaling gave the same result (0.023). Isotonic regression gave 0.032, and its log-likelihood stayed above the others (0.426 against 0.392) even when there was nothing to fix, presumably because a step function spends data on a distortion that a single number can describe.
  • When the problem is something else, it may not. For the overfitted degree-6, 100-point model of Mode 1 (20 samples, 1000 calibration points, 5000 test points, 15 bins) the raw ECE of 0.074 became 0.115 after temperature scaling and 0.100 after Platt scaling, while isotonic regression reached 0.028. At the same time all three improved the log-likelihood (0.628 raw; 0.519, 0.517 and 0.467). A plausible reason, which I did not test separately, is that these methods are fitted to minimise the log-likelihood, which is dominated by a few confident mistakes, so the fitted scale compresses all probabilities and leaves the mid-range predictions too timid (the diagram shows underconfidence after temperature scaling). The Brier score also got worse for temperature and Platt (0.155 to 0.167 and 0.166) and better for isotonic regression (0.147). The temperature fitted varied enormously between samples (7.2 ± 7.8). On XOR data with the same model, the picture was the same (ECE 0.056 raw, 0.077, 0.071 and 0.022). Calibration error is not one number: ECE, the log-likelihood and the Brier score can disagree, so look at all three.
  • How much calibration data? Use the chart ECE vs calibration-set size. For the same overfitted model (10 samples × 20 fresh calibration sets, 10 bins, 2000 test points) isotonic regression’s ECE was 0.092 with 50 calibration points, 0.070 with 100, 0.052 with 200, 0.037 with 500 and 0.029 with 1000, against a raw 0.076, so it needed about 100 points to beat doing nothing on ECE. Its log-likelihood was worse than the raw 0.648 up to 100 points (0.868 and 0.680) and better from 200 (0.573). Platt and temperature scaling improved the log-likelihood only from 0.555 and 0.550 at 100 points to 0.537 and 0.538 at 1000, and their ECE did not improve with more data (0.079 to 0.091 and 0.102 to 0.117).
  • Never fit on the test set. Tick the Cheat box. With 300 points used both to fit and to measure, isotonic regression reached an ECE of 0.000 ± 0.000 against 0.061 ± 0.013 when fitted on separate data; temperature scaling changed only from 0.128 ± 0.047 to 0.113 ± 0.046, because one number can adapt very little. The more flexible the recalibrator, the more it can memorise.

Mode 3: distribution shift

The model and its recalibrator are trained on the source data and then evaluated on data that have changed. Four shifts are offered, each defined so that the page still knows the exact true probability of every shifted point (the true-probability reference below was calibrated to within sampling noise in each case on 200,000 points: its mean prediction was within 0.001 of the share of class 1 and its ECE was at most 0.003).

  • Covariate shift: the inputs move along x, but the labelling rule P(y | x) is exactly the source rule (Shimodaira 2000).
  • Label shift: the share of class 1 becomes the chosen value while what each class looks like is unchanged. The true probability is the source odds multiplied by the ratio of new to old class odds (Saerens, Latinne and Decaestecker 2002); I checked this against the page’s exact mixture calculation, with a largest difference of 3×10−16.
  • Concept shift: the whole class picture moves along x, so the labelling rule changes under the model.
  • Noise growth: every class covariance is multiplied by 1 + the chosen value.

Ovadia et al. (2019) report that post-hoc calibration can look good on data like the training data and still fail under even mild shift; this mode lets you see that on a small example. The numbers below are for the overlapping blobs with a degree-3 model on 300 points, Platt scaling fitted on 500 source points, 10 samples, 2000 target points, 15 bins, top-label unless stated, and (for “target”) 200 labelled target points.

  • Concept shift hurts a lot and source recalibration cannot help. At shifts of 0, 1, 2 and 3 the raw ECE was 0.033, 0.101, 0.283 and 0.407, and Platt scaling fitted on the source data gave 0.038, 0.095, 0.277 and 0.400. Accuracy fell from 81.6% to 74.5%, 60.8% and 53.5% while mean confidence rose from 82.3% to 84.0%, 89.0% and 94.1%. Recalibrating again on 200 labelled target points gave 0.045, 0.054, 0.057 and 0.057, a large improvement, though still above the 0.024 of the true probability.
  • Noise growth. At growth 0, 0.5, 1 and 2 the ECE was 0.033, 0.075, 0.120 and 0.181 (source recalibration 0.038, 0.063, 0.104, 0.167; target recalibration 0.045, 0.055, 0.064, 0.067). Accuracy fell from 81.6% to 69.3% while confidence rose from 82.3% to 87.2%; even the true probability only reaches 70.2% accuracy at growth 2, so most of the fall in accuracy is unavoidable; what goes wrong is that the confidence rises instead of falling.
  • Label shift and the convention. With 5% class 1 the model’s accuracy did not move (symmetric classes), the top-label ECE was only 0.043 and the P(class 1) ECE was 0.225; at 95%, 0.052 against 0.228. The top-label ECE can hide this shift. I did not isolate why; a likely reason is that over- and under-confidence in the predictions of the two classes fall into the same bins and offset each other. On the imbalanced data (20% class 1) shifted to 50% and 80% the P(class 1) ECE went from 0.033 to 0.178 and 0.343, with accuracy falling from 87.2% to 74.5% and 62.2%; recalibrating on 200 target points gave 0.052 and 0.046.
  • Covariate shift was mild in my runs. A degree-3 model on the overlapping blobs, whose true boundary is a line, stayed calibrated (ECE 0.034, 0.034, 0.035 and 0.029 at shifts 0 to 3, while accuracy rose from 81.9% to 93.5%). A degree-2 model on the moons was between 0.044 and 0.057 for shifts up to 3. A degree-3 model on XOR went from 0.028 to 0.037 and 0.058 at shifts 0, 1 and 2, with confidence 4.7 points above accuracy at 2. So covariate shift only breaks calibration when the model is wrong in the region the inputs move into; I did not find a case with dramatic damage.

Mode 4: the forecaster game

You see 150 labelled points and one query point whose class is hidden. You announce a probability from a fixed list (5%, 10%, 20%, …, 90%, 95%); the class is then drawn at random from the exact true probability q of that point and revealed. After many rounds your reliability diagram shows whether your 70%s came true about 70% of the time. Because only 11 distinct forecast values are possible, the Murphy (1973) decomposition of the Brier score is exact:

Brier = REL − RES + UNC
REL = Σk (nk/n)(fk − ok)²   RES = Σk (nk/n)(ok − ō)²   UNC = ō(1 − ō)

where fk is the k-th announced level, nk the number of rounds at that level, ok the observed share of class 1 at it and ō the overall share. Reliability is the calibration error, resolution is how much your forecasts separate cases whose outcomes differ, and uncertainty depends only on the data. The Brier score itself goes back to Brier (1950). The page checks the identity on every update (it matched to 10−15 in a test of 5000 rounds), and I deliberately do not show this decomposition for the binned diagrams in Modes 1 to 3: with continuous probabilities, binning adds a within-bin term and the identity no longer holds exactly.

Why honesty is the best policy. The Brier score and the log loss are strictly proper scoring rules (Gneiting and Raftery 2007): if the true probability is q, announcing f gives an expected Brier score (f − q)² + q(1 − q) and an expected log loss KL(q‖f) + entropy(q), both smallest at f = q. The chart Cost of misreporting averages the extra part over 4000 query points of the overlapping blobs for a forecaster who knows q but announces σ(s · logit q): both curves are exactly zero at s = 1 and grow on either side. The extra Brier score was 0.047 at s = 0.25, 0.013 at 0.5, 0.009 at 2 and 0.024 at 4; the extra log loss was 0.139, 0.049, 0.079 and 0.378. The log loss punishes overconfidence much harder than the Brier score does.

The simulated forecaster plays this strategy for the chosen number of rounds, rounded to the nearest of the 11 levels. In 2000 rounds on the overlapping blobs (averaged over 10 runs) its average Brier score was 0.171, 0.138, 0.125, 0.133 and 0.145 for s = 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2 and 4, against 0.122 to 0.126 for the unrounded true probability, and its reliability term was 0.044, 0.012, 0.001, 0.007 and 0.012. Even at s = 1 the rounding leaves a small reliability term. A constant 50% forecast scored 0.25, and the uncertainty term of this data set is 0.250.

Scores are noisy. A forecaster who always announced the true probability (rounded) had a Brier score of 0.124 ± 0.029 over 50 rounds (300 simulated games), so after a short game a good score does not prove skill, and a poor one does not prove its absence. The benchmark “trained model” is a degree-3 logistic regression with λ = 0.01 on the 150 points you see; “base rate” announces their class-1 share.

Limits

  • Everything is two-dimensional and synthetic; the true probability is known here and never in practice. Real calibration studies must estimate it from labelled data, with the noise shown in the noise-floor chart.
  • Reliability diagrams and ECE look at a model’s predicted confidence only; they say nothing about whether it is accurate, fair for every subgroup, or calibrated for individual cases.
  • The page uses one temperature, two Platt parameters and a plain step function. Other recalibrators and other binning-free estimates exist; I did not implement them.
  • Random samples are small by design. Run New random sample a few times before believing a difference of a point or two.
  • NLL clips probabilities at 10−6; isotonic regression can output exactly 0 or 1, so its log-likelihood depends on that choice.
