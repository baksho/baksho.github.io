Message Passing Lab

A graph neural network layer does the same thing at every node: collect the feature vectors of the neighbours (the messages), aggregate them with sum, mean or max, and update the node. Press Step to watch one layer in three sub-steps: the current features, the messages travelling along the edges, the aggregated result. Every node carries two numbers (x₁, x₂), so you can follow the exact arithmetic. Click a node to see its calculation written out. Try the scenarios in Settings: mean aggregation smooths the graph, sum explodes, and a node only hears from nodes within k hops after k layers.

How it works

A graph neural network keeps one feature vector hv per node. One layer of message passing updates every node from its neighbourhood N(v) in three steps, which are the three sub-steps of the page:

message mu→v = hu (GCN: hu / √(d̃u d̃v))
aggregate av = AGGu ∈ N(v) ∪ {v} mu→v (sum, mean or max)
update h′v = activation(W · av)

Here every node has only two features so that the numbers fit on the node. In a real network the vectors have hundreds of entries, the messages are usually learned functions of both end points, and W is trained, but the structure is the same. This general form is the message-passing framework of Gilmer et al. (2017). The "GCN" option is the layer of Kipf and Welling (2017): a sum over the neighbours and the node itself, where each message is divided by √(d̃u d̃v). The degrees d̃ include the self-loop, and the page uses exactly this rule.

What the page shows

  • One layer = information moves one hop. After k layers a node's features depend only on nodes reachable by a walk of k steps. The "Impulse" scenario starts with a single non-zero node; the non-zero region grows by exactly one hop per layer. I checked this for every start node on all five preset graphs, with and without self-loop (4,716 node-layer cases, no mismatch).
  • Mean smooths. On the preset graph "two clusters joined by a bridge", the relative spread of the features (RMS distance from the average, divided by the RMS size) was 0.705 at the input and 0.673, 0.637, 0.566 and 0.428 after 1, 2, 4 and 8 mean layers. The decay is geometric: with the self-loop, mean aggregation multiplies the deviation from the average by the second-largest eigenvalue modulus of D̃⁻¹Ã per layer in the long run. I checked this on the path (0.888), the ring (0.805) and the tree (0.890): the measured per-layer ratio after 60 layers agrees with the eigenvalue to four digits. Li, Han and Wu (2018) describe the GCN convolution as a form of Laplacian smoothing and show that stacking many layers makes node features hard to tell apart (over-smoothing). In this toy the loss of distinctness is slow for mean and GCN, much faster for max.
  • Sum explodes. With weights fixed at the identity, the sum rule multiplies the feature size by roughly the degree of the neighbourhood at every layer. After 8 layers the RMS feature size had grown by a factor of about 4,700 on the ring and about 37,000 on the two-cluster graph. On the ring every node adds up 3 terms, so the sums grow like 38 = 6,561 times a common smoothed vector, whose length (about 0.7) is smaller than the initial RMS size (about 1). Mean and GCN stay below the input size (0.7 to 0.8 of it after 8 layers in these runs).
  • Max forgets quickly. Starting from the two-group features, the relative spread under max was 0.24 after 2 layers on the cluster graph and exactly 0 after 3: every node holds the same vector, namely the coordinate-wise maximum over the graph. The price of max is that it ignores how many neighbours say something.
  • Without the self-loop, parity matters. On a star, the hub's information reaches the leaves at layer 1, returns to the hub at layer 2, and so on. The receptive-field chart shows the hub seeing 1, 6, 1, 6, … nodes. With the self-loop every node can "wait", so the receptive field only grows.

Sum, mean and max are also the three aggregators compared in "How Powerful are Graph Neural Networks?" (Xu et al., 2019). There, sum can tell apart multisets that mean and max confuse, which is a reason to prefer sum for expressiveness, at the cost of the growth seen above. The expressive power of such layers is bounded by the Weisfeiler–Leman test, as shown in the same paper.

What this is not. The weights W are set by hand and nothing is trained. The scenarios use tiny graphs and 2-dimensional features so that every number can be read. Over-smoothing in trained networks also depends on the weights, the activation and residual connections, so the rates above describe the parameter-free dynamics only. The relative spread is my own simple summary, not a standard metric, and the numbers quoted in this text come from the preset graphs and the default features of the page.

Things to try

  • Select node 3 in the cluster graph and step through layer 1 with mean aggregation. Check the average by hand against the table in the card under the graph.
  • Switch the rule to Sum and watch the numbers grow. Then switch the chart to "Feature size (log)".
  • Use "Edit edges" to cut the bridge between nodes 3 and 4. The two clusters then never mix, and with the default features the relative spread stays at about 0.71 for any number of layers (measured up to 60).
