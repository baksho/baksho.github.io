How it works

What this page is, and is not. The target matrices here are made up, so they can show different spectrum shapes. They are not weight updates taken from a real fine-tuned model, and a real model's spectra have to be measured, not assumed. Real LoRA training also minimises a task loss on data, not the distance to a known matrix.

The setup

A layer has a frozen weight matrix W of size m × n. LoRA learns an update ΔW = B·A with B of size m × r and A of size r × n, so ΔW has rank at most r and only r·(m + n) numbers are trained instead of m·n. B starts at zero and A starts random, so the update starts at exactly zero.

The best possible rank-r update

Suppose the update you would like is a matrix ΔW*. The closest matrix of rank at most r in Frobenius norm is obtained by keeping its r largest singular values and dropping the rest (the Eckart–Young theorem). The squared error is the sum of the squares of the dropped singular values, so the share of energy captured at rank r tells you exactly how good any rank-r update can be. That is the orange spectrum, the middle heat map and the dashed line.

What gradient descent finds

LoRA does not compute an SVD. It trains B and A by gradient descent on ½‖B·A − ΔW*‖². In this demo it ends up close to the SVD optimum, and the blue dots show how: the strongest singular directions are picked up first, then the weaker ones, which gives the staircase-like descent in the training curve for small initial A. This page uses plain gradient descent for clarity; real LoRA fine-tuning usually uses an optimiser such as Adam, which behaves differently.

If the SVD already gives the best answer, why train B and A?

Because in real fine-tuning you never have ΔW*. It would be the difference between the fine-tuned weights and the original ones, and you only get fine-tuned weights after training. All you have is data and a loss, and LoRA's job is to find a good update using only that, while storing and training just r·(m+n) numbers. Computing an SVD of ΔW* would need ΔW* first, and that would mean training the full m × n update, which is exactly the cost LoRA avoids.

So here ΔW* is a stand-in used as a measuring stick. Because we know it, we can compute the best possible rank-r answer (the SVD) and ask how close gradient descent on B and A gets. In real use there is no such reference. Also note that the best rank-r update for a task loss is not necessarily the truncated SVD of the full fine-tuning update: the two optimise different things.

Reading the charts

  • Singular values. Every matrix can be written as a sum of simple rank-1 pieces, each with a strength, its singular value. The bars show those strengths, strongest first. A few tall bars followed by a short tail means the matrix is close to low rank. Orange bars are the pieces a rank-r update can hold; grey bars are lost. The blue dots show the strengths of the pieces the trained B·A has built so far, so you can watch it pick up the strongest pieces first. The vertical axis can be log or linear: log shows the tail, linear shows the head.
  • Energy captured. Energy is the sum of the squared singular values. The curve gives, for each rank, the share of the total energy kept by the best rank-r update. A curve that rises steeply and flattens means a small rank is enough. A slowly rising curve means you need a high rank. The dashed lines mark 90% and 99%, and the numbers panel lists the rank needed for each.
  • Training. The vertical axis is the relative error ‖B·A − ΔW*‖ / ‖ΔW*‖, where 1.0 means no better than a zero update and 0 would be a perfect match. The horizontal axis counts gradient steps on a log scale, so the first steps are visible. The dashed orange line is the best any rank-r update can reach, and the blue curve should settle onto it. With a small initial A the curve sits near 1.0 for a while before dropping.

What the target options mean

Each target is built to have a particular spectrum, and all of them are scaled to the same overall size. For the first three, the singular directions are smooth cosine patterns paired at random, so the heat maps look like interference patterns; the directions do not affect the energy curve, only the spectrum does.

  • Power-law spectrum. Singular values fall as 1/ip. The tail decays slowly, so many weak directions together still carry real energy. A small exponent gives a flat-ish spectrum that needs high rank. A large one is nearly low-rank.
  • Exponential spectrum. Singular values fall as e−i/L. The tail disappears fast, so it is close to exactly low rank, and a modest rank captures almost everything.
  • 4 strong directions + noise. Four large singular values and a small floor spread over all the others. This is a stand-in for "the update really is low rank plus a little residual": the energy jumps up to rank 4 and then climbs very slowly.
  • A picture. A 64 × 64 image with a ring, a diagonal bar and a block on a soft background. Its spectrum is whatever the picture has, and sharp edges need many ranks to sharpen. It is useful for seeing what a given rank keeps visually.
  • Random (no structure). Gaussian random numbers. The spectrum is roughly flat, so no low-rank update does well. It is the worst case for LoRA.

What to try

  • Choose Power-law spectrum and raise the rank: the first few ranks capture most of the energy, then returns shrink.
  • Choose Random (no structure): the spectrum is flat, so low ranks capture little. Low-rank updates only help when the update really is close to low rank.
  • Choose 4 strong directions + noise and compare rank 3, 4 and 5: the energy jumps up to rank 4 and then barely moves.
  • Make the learning rate small and the initial A tiny: training sits near zero for a while before it takes off.

Sizes

The matrices are 64 × 64 so everything runs instantly. In real transformer layers the dimension is often in the thousands, where a small rank is a tiny share of the parameters: the page shows what the same rank would cost at d = 4096.

Why low rank might work in practice

The LoRA authors hypothesise that the updates needed to adapt a language model have a low "intrinsic rank", and report that very small ranks can suffice. Whether that holds depends on the model and the task. A related but different observation is that tuning only a small randomly projected set of parameters can recover most of the full fine-tuning performance for some models. This page does not test either claim.
