What is a neural network?

A neural network is a way of building a program that learns from examples instead of following hand-written rules. It is a collection of small units, called neurons, connected in layers. Each neuron takes in some numbers, combines them, and passes a new number on to the next layer.

Neurons, weights and activations

Every connection carries a weight, a number that says how strongly one neuron should influence the next. A neuron multiplies each incoming value by its weight, adds the results together with a bias, and passes the sum through an activation function such as tanh, ReLU or sigmoid. The activation is what lets a network draw curved boundaries instead of only straight lines. In the diagram above, orange connections are negative, blue ones are positive, and thicker lines mean larger weights.

Choosing an activation function

  • ReLU outputs zero for negative inputs and the input itself otherwise. It is simple and fast, but a neuron whose input is always negative outputs zero everywhere and stops learning. The playground marks such dead neurons with a red dashed outline.
  • Leaky ReLU keeps a small slope (0.01) for negative inputs, so its neurons can recover instead of dying.
  • ELU and Softplus are smooth relatives of ReLU. ELU dips slightly below zero for negative inputs, while Softplus is a smooth curve that stays above zero.
  • Swish multiplies the input by its own sigmoid. It is smooth, dips slightly below zero for small negative inputs, and appears in some modern networks.
  • Tanh and Sigmoid squash their output into a fixed range (−1 to 1, and 0 to 1). They saturate for large inputs, which makes gradients vanish in deep networks.
  • Sine is periodic and produces wave-like patterns. It is sensitive to the learning rate, especially with the squared and product features, so the playground warns you when the setting looks risky.
  • Linear adds no non-linearity, so the whole network behaves like a single linear model.

Features and hidden layers

The inputs on the left are features of each data point: its coordinates (X1, X2) and values computed from them, such as X1² or sin(X1). Hidden layers combine these into new, more useful features, and each small square shows what one neuron responds to across the plane. A well-chosen feature can make a hard problem easy: try the circle dataset with X1² and X2² switched on.

Training

Training means adjusting the weights to reduce a loss, a number that measures how wrong the predictions are. Here the loss is the average squared error. Backpropagation works out how much each weight contributes to the loss, and gradient descent then moves every weight a small step in the direction that lowers it. The learning rate sets the size of that step, and the batch size sets how many examples are averaged for each step. One epoch is one full pass over the training data.

Generalization

A network can fit its training points almost perfectly and still do badly on new ones. That is why the data is split into a training set and a test set: the test loss estimates how well the model works on data it has not seen. Noise and large networks make overfitting easy to see. Regularization (L1 or L2) penalizes large weights, and L1 in particular tends to push weights to exactly zero. Elastic Net mixes the two penalties. Dropout works differently: during training it randomly switches off hidden neurons (greyed out in the diagram while the network runs), so the network cannot rely on any single neuron.

Things to try

  • Solve the spiral dataset with as few neurons as you can.
  • Switch the activation from tanh to ReLU and compare the decision boundaries.
  • Add noise and watch the gap between training and test loss grow.
  • Turn on L1 regularization and watch weights shrink to zero.
  • Hover over any neuron to see what it computes, or edit a weight by hand.
  • Choose ReLU with a high learning rate and look for neurons with a red dashed outline: they are dead.
  • Turn on Dropout and press play to watch neurons switch off at random.
