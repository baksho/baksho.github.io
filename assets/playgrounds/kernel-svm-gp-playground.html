Kernel, SVM and Gaussian-Process Playground

Two methods that use the same kernels. A support vector machine (SVM) separates two classes of points with a boundary and a margin around it; only the support vectors hold it in place. Gaussian-process (GP) regression fits a curve through points and also tells you how sure it is, as a band that narrows near data. How it works

What this page is, and is not. Every data set is made up by the page, and the canvas is a small 2D toy (x from −1.33 to 1.33, y from −1 to 1). The kernel settings below are my own way of writing the kernels, not the only one. The SVM solver is my own small reimplementation of the standard SMO method with second-order working-set selection; I checked it against scikit-learn (below). All numbers quoted come from my own runs of this code on made-up data.

Kernels in one paragraph

A kernel k(x, z) is a similarity score between two points. Using it is equivalent to mapping every point into a space of many more features and taking ordinary dot products there, but the feature space is never built. Three kernels are offered. Linear: x·z (for the GP, 1 + xz, so the line may have an offset). RBF: exp(−γ‖x − z‖²), where a large γ means two points must be very close to count as similar. Polynomial: (γ x·z + 1)degree. The factor γ and the constant 1 in the polynomial kernel are my choices; the textbook form is (σ0² + x·z)p (Rasmussen and Williams 2006, chapter 4), which is the same family up to scaling.

SVM

For two classes labelled +1 and −1, the SVM looks for a function f(x) = Σi αi yi k(xi, x) + b. The solid line is f = 0 (the decision boundary) and the dashed lines are f = ±1 (the margin). It minimises

½‖w‖² + C Σi max(0, 1 − yi f(xi))

The first term makes the margin wide; the second charges for every point that is on the wrong side of its margin line. The idea of a maximum-margin boundary used with kernels is from Boser, Guyon and Vapnik (1992); the version with the penalty C, which allows classes that cannot be separated perfectly, is from Cortes and Vapnik (1995). Most αi end up exactly zero. The points with αi > 0 are the support vectors, drawn with a ring. A thin ring marks a free support vector (0 < α < C), which lies exactly on a margin line. A thick ring marks a bound one (α = C), which lies on or inside the margin or on the wrong side. Every other point could be deleted without changing the boundary: for three cases (two moons with RBF, easy groups with a linear kernel, overlapping groups with RBF) I refitted using only the support vectors and the decision function changed by at most 9.3×10−10 over 2000 test points. Cortes and Vapnik also give a bound relating the expected test error to the expected number of support vectors divided by the number of training points, so fewer support vectors is a good sign.

The page solves the dual problem with sequential minimal optimisation (SMO), choosing the pair of αs to update by second-order information, as in LIBSVM (Fan, Chen and Lin 2005; Chang and Lin 2011). It stops when the largest violation of the optimality conditions is below 10−3. The model is refitted from scratch on every click or drag. The “margin width” in the Results panel is 2/‖w‖. For the linear kernel this is a real width in the picture; for the other kernels ‖w‖ lives in the hidden feature space, so the number is not a distance in the picture.

What to try with the SVM

  • C on easy groups (linear kernel). On “Two groups, easy to separate” (80 points), C = 0.01 puts all 80 points on the bound and gives margin width 4.46; C = 0.1 gives 42 support vectors and width 1.11; C = 1 gives 11 (3 free, 8 bound) and width 0.77; C = 10 gives 3 and width 0.47; from C = 100 to 1000 it stays at 3 free ones and width 0.42, which is the hard-margin solution. Accuracy is 100% throughout, so here C only changes the margin.
  • C on overlapping groups (linear). The margin shrinks from 4.93 (C = 0.01, 80 support vectors, 91.2% training accuracy) to 0.67 (C = 1, 28 support vectors, 93.8%) to 0.30 (C = 1000, 14 support vectors, 93.8%). Large C buys no accuracy here. It does cost time: with a tighter stopping tolerance of 10−6 in this test, the solver needed 40 iterations at C = 0.01 and 18,662 at C = 1000.
  • Choosing the kernel. A straight line cannot separate the curved or XOR data: training accuracy was 92.5% on two moons, 77.5% on the ring around a blob and 82.5% on XOR. RBF (γ = 3, C = 1; type 3 into the box next to the slider) reached 98.8%, 100% and 100%. A polynomial (γ = 2, degree 3 on the moons and degree 2 on the ring and XOR, C = 1) reached 100% on all three.
  • Too flexible. Set RBF γ = 100 and C = 1000. The boundary wraps around single points. I compared it with γ = 3, C = 1 on 20 random training sets of 80 points, testing each on 2000 fresh points of the same kind. On two moons the test accuracy went from 99.4% to 99.0%, with the average number of support vectors rising from 20 to 65. On the overlapping groups it went from 91.2% to 80.8%: training accuracy became 100%, with 72 of 80 points as support vectors. Training accuracy alone would have hidden that.

Gaussian-process regression

Here the kernel decides what kind of curves are likely before seeing any data. A GP says that the curve values at any set of x positions are jointly Gaussian with zero mean and covariance given by the kernel (times σf²). With observed noisy points, conditioning gives a Gaussian again, with a closed form (Rasmussen and Williams 2006, chapter 2):

mean(x*) = k*T (K + σn²I)−1 y
var(x*) = k(x*, x*) − k*T (K + σn²I)−1 k*

The solid line is the mean. The darker band is the mean ± 2 standard deviations of the curve, so it shows what is unknown about the underlying function. The lighter band also adds the noise variance σn², so it shows where a new noisy measurement would fall. The three thin lines are random curves drawn from the posterior, which show shapes the data allow. The “log marginal likelihood” in the Results panel measures how well the kernel and its settings explain the data, balancing fit against complexity; it is higher for a better explanation. Nothing is tuned for you: the sliders are yours to move.

What to try with the GP

  • No data, then a few points. With no points the darker band is exactly ±2σf everywhere (half-width 2.00 with σf = 1). With the first 3 points of the 30-point sine data (RBF, γ = 2, σn = 0.1) the average half-width over x from −1.2 to 1.2 was 1.51; with all 30 points, 0.10.
  • Away from the data. On the 8-point sine data, the half-width at x = 0, inside the data, was 0.14 and at x = 1.33, just beyond the last point, 0.93. The band grows where there is no data: the curve is only trustworthy where points are.
  • Is the band honest? On that 8-point sine sample, the true curve (which I know because I generated it) lay inside the darker band at 23 of 25 evenly spaced x positions. This is one sample, not a calibration test.
  • Kernel width γ. On the 8-point sine data the log marginal likelihood was −17.31, −2.91, −1.77, −6.77 and −8.14 for γ = 0.1, 0.5, 2, 12 and 100, so a middle value explains the data best. With 30 points the best of those five was γ = 0.5 (17.94 against 16.79 at γ = 2). Too small a γ gives stiff curves; too large a γ gives wiggly curves that return to zero between points.
  • Noise σn. On the same 8 points the log marginal likelihood for σn = 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.3 and 1 was −4.57, −0.82, −1.77, −4.63 and −9.69. The points were generated with noise 0.08, and the best of these five was 0.05.
  • Linear kernel. On the “Straight trend” data (10 points) the linear kernel gives a straight mean with a band that is narrowest in the middle (half-width 0.06 at x = 0) and wider at the ends (0.14 and 0.14).
  • A smooth kernel and a jump. On the “Step” data the RBF mean (γ = 2) at x = 0, 0.1 and 0.2 was −0.32, −0.14 and 0.06, whereas the true values were −0.4 before 0.1 and 0.45 after. A smooth kernel cannot make a sharp jump; the choice of kernel is an assumption about the curve.

How I checked it

  • SVM against scikit-learn. In 120 test cases (all kernels, several data sets and settings of C and γ) my solver and scikit-learn's SVC, both at tolerance 10−9, gave the same support vectors, decision values within 9.4×10−6 (relative) and αs within 1.3×10−4 of C. All runs converged and the gap between the primal and dual objective was below 6×10−8.
  • GP against numpy. In 25 cases the mean differed by at most 1.4×10−8, the variance by at most 1.2×10−7 and the log marginal likelihood by at most 1.8×10−6 (5.5×10−7 against scikit-learn). Over 20,000 posterior samples, the sample mean and variance matched the exact values to within 0.0043 and 0.0004.

Limits

  • The page uses a plain Cholesky factorisation for the GP and a full kernel matrix for the SVM, so it is meant for at most a few hundred points. It adds a tiny diagonal term to the GP matrix if the factorisation fails; the Results panel says so when that happens.
  • The SVM needs both classes present before it can fit. The GP always assumes zero mean and fixed hyperparameters; it does not tune them.
  • The GP band is the model’s uncertainty given its assumptions. A bad kernel can give a confident band that is wrong, as the step example shows.
  • One input dimension for the GP and two for the SVM, because that is what can be drawn. Real problems have more dimensions, where the intuition from these pictures is weaker.
