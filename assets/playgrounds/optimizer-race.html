What is happening here?

Every optimizer is trying to find the lowest point of a surface f(x, y). At each step it looks at the gradient ∇f, the direction of steepest ascent, and moves θ = (x, y) a little in the opposite direction. The optimizers differ in how they use that gradient. Below, g is the gradient at the current point, η is the learning rate, and ε = 10−8.

SGD

θ ← θ − η g
The plain step. It is cheap and simple, but it zigzags across narrow valleys and crawls along flat ones.

Momentum

v ← β v + g,  θ ← θ − η v
The velocity v remembers previous gradients, so steady directions build up speed and zigzags partly cancel. Too much momentum overshoots and swings around the minimum.

Nesterov

v ← β v + ∇f(θ − ηβv),  θ ← θ − η v
Momentum that measures the gradient at the point it is about to reach, which lets it brake earlier.

AdaGrad

G ← G + g²,  θ ← θ − η g / (√G + ε)
Each coordinate gets its own step size, shrinking as its squared gradients accumulate. Fast at first, but the steps only ever get smaller.

RMSProp

s ← ρ s + (1−ρ) g²,  θ ← θ − η g / (√s + ε)
Like AdaGrad, but with a moving average, so old gradients are forgotten and the step size can recover.

Adam

m ← β1m + (1−β1)g,  v ← β2v + (1−β2)g²
θ ← θ − η m̂ / (√v̂ + ε)
Momentum on the gradient (m) combined with RMSProp-style scaling (v), where m̂ and v̂ are bias-corrected by dividing by 1−βt.

The surfaces

  • Rosenbrock valley: a long curved valley. Finding the valley is easy; following it to the minimum at (1, 1) is hard.
  • Beale: flat plateaus with steep walls and a minimum at (3, 0.5). Gradients differ by orders of magnitude across the plot.
  • Saddle: a saddle point at the origin with two minima beyond it. Starting near the ridge shows how quickly each optimizer escapes.
  • Narrow valley: a tilted quadratic bowl with one direction 50 times steeper than the other, a standard example of an ill-conditioned problem.
  • Himmelblau: four separate minima. Which one an optimizer reaches depends on the start and on the optimizer.
  • Rastrigin: a regular grid of small dips around one global minimum at (0, 0). Which dip an optimizer settles in depends on where it starts and on its settings.
  • Ackley: a nearly flat outer plain, many small dips, and one deep hole at (0, 0). The gradient far from the centre is tiny, so the choice of optimizer matters a lot.
  • Styblinski–Tang: four minima of different depth. The global one is near (−2.90, −2.90); the other three are shallower local minima.

The 3D view

The View menu switches between the contour map and a 3D landscape of the same function. In 3D, both height and colour show f−f* on a logarithmic scale, so steep walls do not hide the shape of the valley (heights are therefore not proportional to the loss). A trail is drawn faintly where the surface hides it. With Rotate on, drag to turn the surface and scroll to zoom; with Rotate off, click the surface to choose the starting point.

Things to try

  • On the narrow valley, raise the SGD learning rate until it zigzags, then watch how Momentum smooths the same path.
  • On the saddle, start very close to the ridge (y near 0) and compare how long each optimizer takes to leave it.
  • On Himmelblau, click several starting points and see whether all optimizers choose the same minimum.
  • On Rastrigin or Styblinski–Tang, change the starting point slightly and see which minimum each optimizer ends in.
  • Switch to the 3D view and rotate it to look along a valley from the side.
  • Turn on gradient noise to imitate mini-batch SGD and see which optimizers stay on course.
  • Raise a learning rate until an optimizer diverges, then lower it again without resetting.

Gradient noise adds the same random vector to every optimizer's gradient at each step, so the comparison stays fair. All optimizers start from the same point, and each runs with its own learning rate because a good value for one is rarely good for another.
