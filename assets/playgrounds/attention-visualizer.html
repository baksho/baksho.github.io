Attention Visualizer

In a transformer, every word decides how much to look at every other word. That decision is the attention matrix: row i shows how word i spreads one unit of attention over all the words. Type a sentence, choose a head, and change the temperature and the mask to see how the pattern responds. You can use four hand-set heads, each designed to do one readable thing, or switch to a small model and train it yourself from random weights to see what it learns. Use the Settings panel on the right.

How it works

What this page is, and is not. It is a tiny one-layer model. In hand-set mode the four heads are designed so that each has a pattern you can read; nothing is trained. In trained mode the model learns from a made-up grammar and its heads are usually less tidy. Either way the page shows the mechanics of one attention layer, not what a real language model has learned.

Queries, keys and scores

Every word gets two vectors: a query (what this word is looking for) and a key (what this word offers to others). The score of word i for word j is the dot product of the query of i with the key of j, divided by √dk, where dk is the length of the vectors. This is the scaled dot-product attention of Vaswani et al. The division keeps the scores from growing just because the vectors are longer. The row detail below the matrix lists these numbers for the word you select.

Softmax, one row at a time

For each query word the scores over all key words go through a softmax, which turns them into positive weights that sum to 1:

weightj = exp(scorej / τ) / Σk exp(scorek / τ)

These weights are one row of the matrix.

Temperature

A standard transformer uses τ = 1, so the only scaling is the 1/√dk above. The τ slider is an extra knob added here to show what sharpness means. It rescales every score. With a small τ the largest score wins almost all of the weight; with a large τ the weights approach 1/n for n visible words. The "entropy" number in the row detail measures this: 0 bits means all weight is on one word, and log2 n bits means perfectly even.

Masks

A mask removes some word pairs by setting their score to −∞ before the softmax. Their weight is then exactly 0 and the remaining weights are renormalised to sum to 1. A causal mask blocks every word from looking at later words; it is used in models that generate text from left to right, so a word cannot peek at the future. Hiding a word with the × button blocks it as a key for every row, which is how padding positions are ignored. In both cases the matrix keeps its shape and the blocked cells are hatched.

The four hand-set heads (hand-set mode)

  • Previous word. Query and key use only word positions, arranged so that the score is highest for the word one step back.
  • Same word. Each distinct word gets its own orthogonal code (a row of a 16 × 16 Hadamard matrix), so the score is high only between identical words. Try the example with repeated words.
  • Word role. Each word is given a rough class (article, adjective, noun, verb, pronoun, preposition, conjunction, other) from a small word list and a few suffix rules. A hand-written table says which classes look for which, for example verbs look for nouns and pronouns. A small distance penalty prefers nearby words. Because the classes come from a short list, some words will be misclassified (the tags under the tokens show what the head sees).
  • Nearby words. Positions only again: the score falls with distance from the word itself.

Real transformers run many heads like this in parallel, each with its own learned query and key rules, and combine their outputs.

Hand-set or trained

The Model option switches between the four hand-set heads above and a small transformer that you train here, starting from random weights. Nothing is trained until you press play; reset goes back to fresh random weights, and "Try another random start" picks a different set.

Task. The model reads a sentence and, at every position, predicts the next word. The sentences come from a made-up grammar with a 36-word vocabulary (a few templates such as "the big cat chased the dog and the cat barked ." and "the cat sat on the mat because it was tired ."). The task and the grammar are my own choices for this page, not a standard benchmark. The loss is the average negative log-probability of the true next word (lower is better).

Model and training. One layer, four heads of size 4, model width 16, learned word and position embeddings, no feed-forward block and no layer normalisation. It is trained with a causal mask and τ = 1, using the Adam optimiser (Kingma and Ba) with a learning rate that starts at 0.02 and decays, batches of 16 sentences and 3000 steps. A few percent of input words are randomly replaced by <unk>, so that unknown words in your own sentence are not completely foreign.

The chart shows the loss on a fixed set of 120 held-out sentences. The dashed line is a baseline that sees only the current word (it counts which word follows which); attention can only beat it by using earlier words. The copy test measures, at the position after "and the", how much probability goes to the nouns that appeared earlier in the sentence, which is where the grammar repeats one of them.

What I measured (my own runs of this code, 3000 steps; your numbers will vary a little): the loss falls from about 3.7 to about 1.0, below the 1.5 of the current-word-only baseline, and the copy test reaches roughly 0.9 or higher. Which head does the copying differs from one random start to the next, and it is sometimes shared by two or three heads. The learned heads are usually mixtures, for example part previous-word and part first-word, rather than one clean rule each, so the head descriptions in trained mode are percentages measured on the held-out sentences, not labels I chose.

What real heads look like

Analyses of trained models find some heads with simple, readable behaviour and others that are broad or hard to describe. Clark et al. report that BERT heads show patterns such as attending to fixed positional offsets, to delimiter tokens, or broadly across the sentence, and that some heads line up with syntactic relations and coreference. Voita et al. found that in a translation model a small number of specialised heads do most of the work, and many others can be removed with little loss. The previous-word, same-word and word-role heads here are cartoons of these kinds of behaviour, not copies of them.

Limits

  • This is one layer with four heads. In hand-set mode the weights come from rules; in trained mode they come from the made-up grammar only.
  • The trained model has seen only sentences from that grammar. Words outside its 36-word vocabulary are read as <unk>, and other sentences you type are outside what it learned, so its guesses and attention there are not meaningful. It was trained with the causal mask, so the unmasked view is also outside its training.
  • The matrix is only half of an attention layer. In a full layer the weights are used to take a weighted average of value vectors, which this page leaves out.
  • Attention weights show where one head reads from; on their own they are not an explanation of a model's output.
  • Sentences are cut at 14 tokens, and the word classes come from a short word list, so unusual words default to "noun".
