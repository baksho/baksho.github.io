Bayesian Updating Lab

Bayesian inference keeps a distribution over the unknown quantity instead of a single estimate. You start with a prior (what you believed before), multiply by the likelihood (how well each value explains the data) and renormalise to get the posterior. For three classic models every step has a closed form, so this page shows the exact curves, not an approximation: the bias of a coin, the mean of a Gaussian, and a regression line. Add data, change the prior, and see how many observations it takes to overrule a wrong prior. Choose the mode at the top of Settings.

How it works

Everything on this page is computed in your browser, and every curve is exact: for each of the three models the prior and the likelihood combine into a posterior of the same family (a conjugate pair), so nothing is sampled or approximated. Only the random lines in Mode 3 and the simulated data are random.

The one rule behind all three modes

posterior(θ) ∝ prior(θ) × likelihood(data | θ)

The grey likelihood curve says how well each value of the unknown explains the data. Multiplying it by the prior and rescaling to area 1 gives the posterior. With a flat prior the posterior equals the (rescaled) likelihood, which is why the grey and blue curves coincide in the default coin scenario.

Mode 1: the coin (Beta prior)

  • Prior. Beta(a, b) with a = (prior mean) × (strength) and b = (1 − prior mean) × (strength). It acts like strength = a + b imaginary flips: a heads and b tails seen before the first real flip.
  • Update. With h heads and t tails the posterior is Beta(a + h, b + t). The data only add to the counts, so the order of the flips does not matter and updating one flip at a time gives the same posterior as updating once.
  • Prior against data. The posterior mean is (a + h) / (a + b + n), a weighted average of the prior mean and h/n. The data get the weight n / (n + strength). At n = strength the prior and the data count equally.
  • MAP and mean. The MAP is the highest point of the posterior, (A − 1) / (A + B − 2) for A = a + h and B = b + t. It differs from the mean and is undefined or sits at an end when A or B is at most 1. The 95% interval shown is the equal-tailed one: 2.5% of the posterior probability on each side.

Mode 2: the mean of a Gaussian (known noise)

  • Each measurement is the unknown mean plus noise with a known standard deviation σ. The prior is Normal(μ₀, τ₀²).
  • The posterior is Normal again. Precisions (1 / variance) add: 1/τ₀² + n/σ². The posterior mean is the precision-weighted average of the prior mean and the sample mean, with weight nτ₀² / (nτ₀² + σ²) on the data. Because a Gaussian is symmetric, its MAP equals its mean.
  • A new measurement has two sources of uncertainty, the noise σ and what is still unknown about the mean, so the predicted variance is σ² + τₙ².

Mode 3: the regression line

  • The model is y = w₀ + w₁x + noise, with known noise σ and independent Normal(0, τ²) priors on both weights.
  • The posterior over (w₀, w₁) is a two-dimensional Gaussian with precision matrix I/τ² + ΦᵗΦ/σ² and mean (posterior covariance) × Φᵗy/σ², where Φ has a row (1, x) per point. The heat map shows this density; the thin lines in the main plot are draws from it.
  • The posterior mean is exactly the ridge regression solution with penalty σ²/τ². So the MAP line is ridge regression, and the posterior adds the uncertainty that ridge regression leaves out.
  • The prediction variance at x is σ² + φᵗ(posterior covariance)φ with φ = (1, x). It is smallest near the data and grows away from them.

What the plots mean

  • Running estimates compare the posterior mean with the raw estimate (h/n or the sample mean). Early on the posterior is pulled towards the prior mean; later the two agree.
  • Weight of the data is the share of the posterior mean that comes from the data rather than from the prior.
  • Flips needed to overrule the prior uses the expected data: the posterior mean is then (prior mean × s + nθ) / (s + n), so its distance to the true θ is s · |prior mean − θ| / (s + n). Requiring this to be at most ε = 0.05 gives n ≥ s(|prior mean − θ| / ε − 1). For a prior mean of 0.8 and θ = 0.3 that is n ≥ 9s. In the Gaussian mode the same argument gives n ≥ (σ²/τ₀²)(|μ₀ − μ| / ε − 1) with ε = 0.1.

Credible intervals and a wrong prior (coin). A 95% credible interval is a statement about your belief under the model. To see how it behaves if the model is wrong, I fixed the true θ = 0.3 and counted, over 4000 simulated data sets per cell, how often the 95% interval contained 0.3:

Read this carefully. These are frequencies at one fixed true value, which is a frequentist question. A credible interval only promises 95% on average if the true value really is drawn from the prior. A confident wrong prior produces narrow intervals that miss the truth almost every time at small n, and 300 flips still leave it missing 42% of the time here. The posterior is only as trustworthy as the prior and the model.

Shrinkage in Gaussian measurements. The weight on the data, nτ₀² / (nτ₀² + σ²), for σ = 1: with τ₀ = 3 it is 0.900 after one measurement and 0.978 after five; with a confident prior τ₀ = 0.5 it is 0.200, 0.556, 0.833 and 0.962 after 1, 5, 20 and 100 measurements.

Checks I ran

  • The special functions behind the Beta distribution (log-gamma, the Beta cdf and its quantiles for the credible intervals) agree with SciPy to about 1e-14 on the test points.
  • For all three models, the closed-form posteriors agree with a brute-force posterior computed by multiplying prior and likelihood on a fine grid and normalising, which uses none of the conjugate formulas. Wherever the grid covers the whole distribution and is fine enough, the differences in mean and spread are at rounding level (about 1e-15). The one exception is the coin with a U-shaped Beta, whose spike at the end needed a change of variable and still differs by 2.5e-7. My first, coarser grids showed larger differences (up to 0.1 in a covariance); in each case I traced this to the grid (too narrow, or too coarse for a spike) and not to the formulas.
  • A second implementation of the regression formulas in NumPy matches to 4e-16. Updating one point at a time, and updating in reversed order, gives the same posterior as updating all at once (differences below 3e-16).
  • For data generated and clicked in the page itself, the numbers shown by the page match brute-force grids (coin mean exactly, interval ends to about 1e-6, the grid resolution; Gaussian and regression to rounding).

What this page leaves out

  • The noise level σ is assumed known. With unknown noise the posterior for the mean is no longer Gaussian (it involves a Student-t distribution); this page does not cover that.
  • Only one-parameter and two-parameter conjugate models. Most real models have no closed form and need approximation such as MCMC or variational inference.
  • No model comparison: the page does not compute the marginal likelihood, so it cannot tell you which prior is better supported by the data.
  • Simulated data come from exactly the model the page assumes. Real data rarely do, and the interval table above shows what a wrong assumption costs.
